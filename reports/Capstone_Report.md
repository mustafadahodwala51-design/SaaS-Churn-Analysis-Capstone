# SaaS Churn Analysis - Capstone Report

**Prepared as a data analytics capstone project**

> **All data in this report is synthetic.** The dataset is generated by
> `data/generate_dataset.py` and simulates a subscription business over
> January 2023 to December 2024. It does not describe any real company,
> customer, or revenue. Findings are *associations observed in simulated data*,
> not causal conclusions.

---

## 1. Executive summary

The business grew quickly through 2023 and then hit a ceiling.

Monthly recurring revenue rose from **$50,390 in January 2023** to a peak of
**$1,052,669 in October 2024**, but the last six months of data average only
**+0.8% growth a month** against **+51.9%** early in the period. Active customers
peaked at **846 in September 2024** and fell to **820** by December. MRR looks
flat only because new customers almost exactly replace the ones lost: over the
final six months the business added 140 customers and lost 143.

Three findings matter more than the headline.

**Revenue quality is deteriorating.** The average new customer was worth $1,349
in the first half of 2023 and $567 in the second half of 2024, a 58% fall. The
Starter share of new customers rose from 32% to 48%. The company is not
signing fewer customers - it is signing customers worth well under half of what
it signed before. This is why MRR stalled while logo counts held up.

**The existing base is shrinking.** Net Revenue Retention is **90.4%**. The
business gave back 16.4 percentage points of MRR through churn and recovered
only 6.8 points through expansion. Every new customer partly masks a departing
one.

**Churn is concentrated where it is cheapest to fix.** 38% of all cancellations
happen before a customer has been subscribed for three months. Months 0-3 lose
17 points of the customer base. The first 90 days, not the long tail, are the
problem.

A rule-based risk model identifies **273 active accounts holding $508,458 of
MRR - 49% of active recurring revenue** - that show the warning signs
associated with leaving. That is the working list for a retention campaign, and
the number any such campaign should be measured against.

## 2. The data

| | |
|---|---|
| Period | January 2023 - December 2024 (24 months) |
| Customers | 1,250 |
| Customer-months | 13,815 |
| Plans | Starter, Growth, Business, Enterprise |
| Regions | North America, Europe, Asia Pacific, Latin America |
| Channels | Organic, Paid Search, Referral, Partner, Outbound Sales, Events |

The raw files were deliberately seeded with realistic defects so the cleaning
step has genuine work to do. Twelve categories of issue were found and each was
given one deliberate treatment, documented in `python/data_analysis.py`.

| Issue | Rows | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Duplicate rows | 54 | Dropped, keeping the first per `customer_id` |
| Mixed casing and stray whitespace | throughout | Stripped, upper-cased for grouping, title-cased for display |
| `churn_date` in `DD/MM/YYYY` among ISO dates | 27 | Parsed day-first, per spelling |
| `mrr_at_signup` stored as text with `$` and commas | many | Symbols stripped, cast to numeric |
| Missing `seats`, `nps_score`, `discount_pct` | various | Imputed from a stated fallback, flagged in a new column |
| Negative MRR | 2 | Treated as entry errors, set to null |
| `adoption_rate` on a 0-100 scale among 0-1 values | many | Values above 1 divided by 100 |
| Seat counts above 10,000 | 2 | Reset and flagged rather than deleted silently |
| `EMEA` and `Europe` for one region | 7 | Consolidated into `Europe` |

### The cleaning bug worth documenting

The mixed date format was the most consequential defect, and the failure was
silent. `churn_date` appears in two spellings: `2024-10-01` and `01/08/2023`.
Parsed with pandas' `format="mixed"`, the ambiguous values are read
**month-first regardless of `dayfirst=True`**, so `01/08/2023` - 1 August -
became **8 January**. Nothing raised an error. The symptom was 137 of 430
cancellations appearing in January 2023 and only 15 distinct cancellation months
across the whole dataset.

It was caught by cross-checking each cancellation date against the customer's
last active month in the monthly activity table, which must be exactly one month
earlier. The first version of that check reported **382 mismatches out of 430**.
Parsing each spelling with its own explicit format brings it to **0**, and the
check now runs as part of the pipeline and warns loudly if it regresses.

The same class of bug hid in two other places: a strict `"%Y-%m-%d"` parse
silently nulled all 1,250 `last_login_date` values because the stored values
carried a time part, and a `reindex` against a month key matched on values
rather than position, broadcasting one month's churn count across every row and
zeroing the monthly churn series entirely.

> **Lesson:** with messy data, validate the *result*, not the absence of an
> exception. Every one of these produced a clean run and plausible-looking
> output. Only a cross-check against an independent source - here, the monthly
> activity table - exposed them.

## 3. Methodology

### Retention is a survival calculation, not a share of the base

The most common error in churn analysis is dividing cancellations by everyone
who ever signed up. That makes the retention curve collapse to zero at the
right-hand edge purely because the data stops there.

Here a customer is **at risk** in month *t* only if they were still subscribed
after month *t*. Customers still active at the end of the period are
**censored**: counted as at risk, never as churn. This yields a base of 1,250 at
month 0 falling to 820 at month 23, matching the December 2024 active count
exactly.

### Cohort averages use only observable cohorts

A cohort that signed up three months ago has not been *tested* at month 12.
Averaging it in as 0% says it lost everyone, which is untrue. Each cohort
average therefore uses only the cohorts that lived long enough to reach the
month in question, and the retention heatmap leaves unobserved cells blank
rather than colouring them as total attrition.

| Month of tenure | Cohorts eligible | Average retention |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 23 | 95% |
| 3 | 21 | 86% |
| 6 | 18 | 79% |
| 12 | 12 | 68% |

The cohort count falling from 23 to 12 is the honest signal that the late-2024
cohorts are simply unobserved, not that they are failing.

### Tenure is recomputed, not trusted

`tenure_months` is rebuilt from the distinct active months in the monthly table
and cross-checked against the reported value. Tenure drives the retention curve,
the risk score, and lifetime value, so an error there propagates everywhere.

### Two independent implementations, reconciled

The analysis is implemented twice - once in Python, once in SQL (DuckDB) - and
`python/validate_sql.py` compares them measure by measure: headline KPIs, the
monthly churn total, the full 24-month survival curve, all 600 cohort retention
cells, churn by plan tier, the MRR trend, NRR, and every one of the 820
per-account risk scores. They agree exactly.

That check earned its place repeatedly. The SQL version originally reported NRR
of **108%** because an inner join dropped churned customers from the NRR
denominator - the same class of error as the mixed-date bug, in a different
language. Correcting that exposed two more that no headline total would have
caught:

- **The risk model was inverted.** Three protective signals were flipped to
  `1 - x` *and* given a negative weight, so the two cancelled and the ranking
  ran backwards. The band sizes stayed 274/273/273 and the bands stayed in
  ascending order, so every summary number looked reasonable - only the
  per-account scores were wrong.
- **`final_mrr` meant two different things.** Python averaged MRR over each
  account's last three active months, as its own column comment documented; the
  SQL took the last single month. That silently moved 515 of 820 accounts and
  changed which MRR sat in the high-risk band.

Both were found by comparing the two implementations, and neither would have
been found by reading the code or by checking a total. The validator now
compares per-account scores and asserts the *ordering* of the risk bands, since
an inverted weight still produces three correctly-sized, correctly-ordered
buckets.

## 4. Findings

### 4.1 Growth has stopped

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| MRR, Jan 2023 | $50,390 |
| MRR peak | $1,052,669 (Oct 2024) |
| MRR, Dec 2024 | $1,051,252 |
| Change since peak | −0.1% |
| Average MoM growth, early period | +51.9% |
| Average MoM growth, last 6 months | +0.8% |
| Active customers peak | 846 (Sep 2024) |
| Active customers, Dec 2024 | 820 (−3.1%) |
| Added vs lost, last 6 months | +140 / −143 |

Monthly cancellations have risen from single digits early in 2023 to as many as
31 in a month by late 2024. That is not necessarily deteriorating - there are far
more customers to lose - but it means the flat MRR line is a ceiling, not
stability. There is no growth left to absorb a bad month.

### 4.2 New business is worth much less than it was

| Period | New customers | Avg MRR per customer | Avg seats | Starter share |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| H1 2023 | 364 | $1,349 | 40.1 | 32% |
| H2 2024 | 140 | $567 | 17.1 | 48% |

In absolute terms, 2023 added 792 customers worth $935,939 against 145
cancellations; 2024 added 458 worth $324,330 against 285 cancellations. The
company signed 42% fewer customers in 2024, and each one was worth 58% less.

**Why it matters:** chasing more small logos will not fix this. The average new
customer would have to be worth roughly what it was in early 2023 simply to
replace the revenue lost to churn.

### 4.3 Who churns - and who it costs

| Plan tier | Customers | Churned | Churn rate | Share of MRR lost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Starter | 493 | 198 | **40.2%** | 10.9% |
| Growth | 373 | 130 | 34.9% | 20.4% |
| Business | 267 | 75 | 28.1% | **40.7%** |
| Enterprise | 117 | 27 | 23.1% | 28.1% |

**These are two different questions and they point at different segments.**
Starter loses the most customers; Business costs the most money. A business that
optimises for the churn *rate* fixes the wrong problem.

| Channel | Customers | Churn rate | Avg discount | Avg LTV |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Organic | 282 | **24.1%** | 3.1% | $15,331 |
| Referral | 232 | 27.6% | 6.8% | $14,899 |
| Outbound Sales | 146 | 36.3% | 13.7% | $11,415 |
| Partner | 196 | 40.3% | 16.4% | $10,124 |
| Paid Search | 281 | 41.6% | 10.2% | $15,083 |
| Events | 113 | **43.4%** | 11.8% | **$9,699** |

The pattern is consistent: the channels that discount hardest to win a deal are
the ones that lose it soonest. Events has both the highest churn and the lowest
lifetime value of any channel.

### 4.4 When customers leave

| Tenure month | At risk | Cancelled | Cancellation rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1,250 | 63 | **5.04%** |
| 1 | 1,177 | 62 | **5.27%** |
| 2 | 1,101 | 39 | 3.54% |
| 3 | 1,041 | 37 | 3.55% |
| 6 | 874 | 23 | 2.63% |
| 12 | 539 | 9 | 1.67% |
| 15 | 361 | 14 | **3.88%** |

The curve is not monotonic, and the shape is the finding. It is worst in months
0-1, falls steadily to a trough of 1.67% at month 12, then climbs back to 3.88%
by month 15 as first annual contracts come up for renewal. Two distinct problems
needing two different interventions: onboarding, then renewal management.

Reliability falls away after month 18. Only 201 customers remain at risk by then,
and months 19-23 swing between 0% and 3.5% on denominators of 147 or fewer.
Those points should not be read as signal.

Survival against the full sign-up base: **70% at month 6, 43% at month 12.**
Median tenure before cancellation is 5 months.

### 4.5 Early warning signals

Comparing the final three months of churned customers against customers who
stayed:

| Signal | Churned | Active | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seat adoption | 33.6% | 38.5% | −4.9 pp |
| Support tickets / month | 1.34 | 1.22 | +0.12 |
| NPS (0-10) | 6.85 | 7.07 | −0.22 |
| Signup discount | 10.5% | 9.0% | +1.5 pp |

All four are recorded **before** the cancellation, so they can flag an account
while there is still time to save it.

> **This is an association, not a cause.** Low adoption may be a symptom of a
> customer who was already planning to leave rather than a reason for leaving.
> Nothing in this dataset establishes direction, and no intervention was tested.

### 4.6 Net Revenue Retention

| | |
|---|---|
| MRR, Dec 2023 (starting cohort of 647 customers) | $836,792 |
| MRR, Dec 2024 from that same cohort | $756,792 |
| **Net Revenue Retention** | **90.4%** |
| of which expansion from seat growth | +6.8 pp |
| of which churn from cancellations | −16.4 pp |

NRR below 100% means the existing base shrank. The company gave back more
revenue through churn than it gained from expansion. NRR is the number that
distinguishes a business getting healthier from one buying enough new customers
to stand still.

### 4.7 Revenue at risk

A rule-based score over six signals, each rescaled to 0-1, with the **sign of
the weight carrying the direction** - a negative weight means a high value is
protective.

| Signal | Weight | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| `final_adoption` | −2.0 | low seat use pushes score up |
| `final_nps` | −1.2 | low NPS pushes score up |
| `final_tickets` | +1.0 | more tickets pushes score up |
| `final_mrr` | +1.0 | bigger account, more MRR at stake |
| `tenure_months` | −0.8 | short tenure pushes score up |
| `discount_pct` | +0.6 | heavy discount pushes score up |

| Risk band | Accounts | MRR | Share of active MRR | Adoption | NPS |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Low | 274 | $266,638 | 25.5% | 66% | 8.3 |
| Medium | 273 | $268,719 | 25.7% | 34% | 7.1 |
| **High** | **273** | **$508,458** | **48.7%** | **15%** | **5.8** |

The bands separate cleanly: high-risk accounts use 15% of their seats and give
NPS 5.8, against 66% and 8.3 for low-risk. This is a ranking tool, not a
validated probability of churn - it was chosen for explainability so a
customer-success manager can see exactly why an account was flagged.

## 5. Recommendations

**1. Fix the first 90 days.** *Owner: Onboarding · Immediate*
38% of all cancellations happen before month 3, and months 0-3 lose 17 points of
the base - the worst window in the curve. Move onboarding, activation and the
first support contact into a structured 90-day programme instead of reactive
support. Highest leverage item on this list, because it addresses the largest
share of all churn.

**2. Start the renewal conversation early.** *Owner: Renewals · Immediate*
Cancellation falls to a 1.7% trough at month 12, then climbs back to 3.9% by
month 15 as first annual contracts come up for decision. Begin that
conversation 90 days before the anniversary, not when the cancellation notice
arrives.

**3. Close the monthly-versus-annual billing gap.** *Owner: Pricing · 1-2 quarters*
Monthly billing churns at 37.3% against 28.9% on annual - an 8.5 point gap.
Offer an annual-billing incentive at signup. Note this is an association in the
data; the gap may partly reflect customers who choose monthly *because* they
expect to leave.

**4. Tie discounting to retention potential.** *Owner: Sales ops · 1-2 quarters*
Churned customers received 1.5 percentage points more discount on average, and
discount-heavy channels churn hardest. Approve discounts against company size
and against the plan the account can realistically grow into.

**5. Re-balance acquisition spend.** *Owner: Marketing · 1-2 quarters*
Events has the highest churn (43.4%) and the lowest lifetime value of any
channel ($9,699); Organic keeps customers at 24.1% with a $15,331 LTV. Shift
budget, and gate renewal of channel budget on a 90-day retention check.

**6. Run a targeted save programme, not a blanket discount.**
*Owner: Customer success · Immediate*
$508,458 of MRR sits with 273 identifiable high-risk accounts. Work that list
directly. A company-wide discount campaign would cost more than the churn it
prevents, and would not reach the accounts that are actually at risk.

**7. Fix the revenue quality problem at the top of the funnel.**
*Owner: Marketing + Sales · 1-2 quarters*
The average new customer is worth 58% less than in early 2023. Reporting
new MRR, new ARR and average deal value alongside new logo count would make
this visible; logo count alone reports growth while revenue per customer
collapses.

## 6. Limitations

1. **The data is simulated.** These findings demonstrate the method. They are
   not evidence about any real business.
2. **Associations, not causes.** Every relationship reported here is
   observational. No causal claim is made or supported.
3. **No experimental design.** There is no control group, so no intervention can
   be shown to have caused an improvement. The recommendations are hypotheses
   to test, not proven fixes.
4. **Late cohorts are unobserved.** Cohorts from late 2024 have not reached
   month 12. Their retention is unknown, not good, and is left blank throughout.
5. **Churn reasons are self-reported** and therefore indicative only.
6. **The risk score is rule-based**, selected for explainability over predictive
   accuracy. It ranks accounts; it does not estimate churn probability, and it
   has not been validated out of sample.
7. **Churn is treated as final.** Real subscriptions include reactivation,
   upgrades and downgrades, none of which this dataset models. A customer who
   leaves and returns is counted as churned.
8. **Two years is a short window.** A single renewal cycle is visible, so
   multi-year retention behaviour cannot be assessed.
9. **The tail of the survival curve is thin.** After month 18 fewer than 201
   customers remain at risk, so late-tenure cancellation rates are noisy and
   should not be interpreted.
